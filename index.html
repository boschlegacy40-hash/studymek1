Guyana: History & Independence

Key events that shaped the nation

Before colonisation

Long before Europeans arrived, Guyana was home to Indigenous peoples including the Arawak, Carib, Wapishana, Warrau, Akawaio, Patamona, Macushi and Wai Wai, who lived along the coast, rivers and interior forests. Many of these groups' descendants remain part of Guyana today, and their place names and traditions still shape the country.

Colonial rule: Dutch, then British

From the late 1500s, Dutch traders and settlers established outposts along the Essequibo, Demerara and Berbice rivers, eventually forming three separate colonies. Enslaved Africans were forcibly brought to work on plantations, and after the abolition of slavery in the 1830s, indentured labourers — mostly from India, along with Portuguese and Chinese workers — were brought in to work the sugar estates. Britain took control of the three Dutch colonies in the early 1800s and united them in 1831 as British Guiana.

The road to independence

Through the 20th century, movements for self-government grew, led by figures such as Cheddi Jagan and Forbes Burnham, who both played major roles in the territory's political development. British Guiana gradually gained more control over its own affairs, moving through stages of internal self-government before full independence was negotiated with Britain.

Independence Day — 26 May 1966

British Guiana officially became the independent nation of Guyana on 26 May 1966, with Forbes Burnham as the country's first Prime Minister. Independence Day is celebrated annually as one of Guyana's most important national holidays.

Becoming a Republic — 23 February 1970

Guyana became a republic on 23 February 1970, replacing the British monarch as head of state with a President while remaining part of the Commonwealth. This date is now celebrated as Republic Day (also called Mashramani, or "Mash," a national festival celebrating Guyanese culture and achievement).

Guyana today

Modern Guyana is known for its multicultural population, vast rainforests, rich biodiversity, and — since the discovery of major offshore oil reserves in the 2010s — a rapidly growing economy. Its motto, "One People, One Nation, One Destiny," reflects the country's diverse heritage of Indigenous, African, Indian, Portuguese, Chinese and European ancestry.

The 10 Administrative Regions

Since 1980, Guyana has been divided into 10 administrative regions for local government. Most region names come from the rivers that mark their borders, and each has its own regional capital.

How local government works: RDCs, NDCs & town councils

Each of the 10 regions is run day-to-day by a Regional Democratic Council (RDC), headed by an elected Regional Chairman. Regions are then broken down into smaller Local Authority Areas, of which there are 80 in total:

  • 10 towns (municipalities) — Georgetown, Linden, New Amsterdam, Rose Hall, Corriverton, Anna Regina, Bartica, Mahdia, Lethem and Mabaruma — each run by a Mayor and Town/City Council.
  • 70 Neighbourhood Democratic Councils (NDCs) — cover villages and rural communities outside the towns (for example Buxton/Foulis, Mocha/Arcadia and Triumph/Beterverwagting in Region 4, or Black Bush Polder in Region 6). Each NDC is run by an elected Chairperson and councillors who look after local roads, drainage, garbage collection and community services.
  • Amerindian Villages — many Indigenous communities are organised separately as titled villages, each led by an elected Toshao (village leader) and Village Council.

At the national level, all of this is overseen by the Ministry of Local Government and Regional Development. As of 2026, the Minister of Local Government and Regional Development is the Hon. Priya Manickchand, supported by Minister within the Ministry Pauline Sukhai. The last Local Government Elections were held in June 2023, when Guyanese voters elected 1,220 councillors across 610 constituencies in the 80 towns and NDCs.

Leaders of Guyana since independence

Guyana has had 10 Presidents since the office was created in 1970. Two — Forbes Burnham and Cheddi Jagan — died while in office.

1970–1980Arthur Chung — 1st President (a ceremonial head of state; Forbes Burnham led the government as Prime Minister)
1980–1985Forbes Burnham — 2nd President; had earlier led Guyana to independence as its first Prime Minister (1964–1980); died in office
1985–1992Desmond Hoyte — 3rd President; moved Guyana toward a more open, market-based economy
1992–1997Cheddi Jagan — 4th President; won Guyana's first internationally recognised free and fair election since 1964; died in office
1997Sam Hinds — served briefly as acting President after Jagan's death
1997–1999Janet Jagan — 6th President; Cheddi Jagan's widow; Guyana's first woman President; resigned due to ill health
1999–2011Bharrat Jagdeo — 7th President; now serves as Vice President under Irfaan Ali
2011–2015Donald Ramotar — 8th President
2015–2020David Granger — 9th President; first President from the APNU coalition
2020–presentIrfaan Ali — 10th and current President; re-elected to a second term in the September 2025 general election. Prime Minister and First Vice President Mark Phillips and Vice President Bharrat Jagdeo serve alongside him.

🗿 Guyana Monuments & Landmarks

Kaieteur Falls

On the Potaro River in Kaieteur National Park, this is one of the world's most powerful single-drop waterfalls — about 226 metres (741 ft) high, roughly four and a half times the height of Niagara Falls. Its Patamona name means "old man falls."

The 1763 Monument ("Cuffy")

Standing in the Square of the Revolution, Georgetown, this bronze statue by sculptor Philip Moore honours Cuffy, who led the 1763 Berbice Rebellion of enslaved Africans. Unveiled in 1976 for Guyana's 10th independence anniversary, Cuffy is Guyana's first National Hero.

Stabroek Market

Opened in 1881, this iron-and-steel market with its landmark clock tower stands on Water Street in Georgetown and is still the city's largest and busiest market today.

St. George's Cathedral

Opened in 1892 and standing 43.5 metres tall, this Anglican cathedral is one of the tallest wooden buildings in the world — built almost entirely of timber.

Umana Yana

A cone-shaped, palm-thatched benab built in 1972 by Wai-Wai Indigenous builders without a single nail. "Umana Yana" means "meeting place of the people" and it still hosts exhibitions and conferences today.

The Public Buildings (Parliament)

Completed in 1834, this stately colonial-era building on Brickdam has housed Guyana's National Assembly since colonial times and remains the seat of Guyana's Parliament today.

Demerara Harbour Bridge

Opened in 1978, this floating toll bridge across the Demerara River was once one of the longest floating bridges in the world. It closed in October 2025 and has been replaced by the new high-level Demerara River Bridge.

🌍 Our Peoples & Culture

The story of "One People, One Nation, One Destiny"

Mashramani — held every 23 February on Republic Day — brings all of Guyana's peoples together in one big street parade.

Guyana's motto is "One People, One Nation, One Destiny." Indigenous peoples were here first, for thousands of years — everyone else is descended from people who arrived later from somewhere else, some brought here against their will, others who came seeking a new life. Here's the story of each group, and what they gave to Guyana.

🪶

Indigenous Peoples (Amerindians)

Guyana's first peoples: Unlike every other group on this page, Indigenous peoples didn't "arrive" in Guyana — they were already here. Archaeological evidence shows people have lived in what is now Guyana for at least 7,000 years, having migrated from the Amazon Basin long before Christopher Columbus sighted the coast in 1498 or any European colonisation began.

The nine nations: Guyana recognises nine Indigenous nations: the Warao, Lokono (Arawak) and Kalina (Carib), who live mainly on or near the coast (Regions 1 and 2), and the Akawaio, Patamona, Makushi, Wapishana, Pemon (Arekuna) and Wai-Wai, who live in the interior (mainly Regions 7, 8 and 9). Together they make up about 78,500 people — roughly 10.5% of Guyana's population, and the majority in Regions 1 and 9.

Their contribution to Guyana: The very name "Guyana" comes from an Indigenous word meaning "land of many waters." Amerindians are credited with inventing the canoe, and with cassava-based cooking and cassareep — the base of pepperpot, Guyana's national dish. Rivers and places across the country — Essequibo, Demerara, Mazaruni, Kaieteur, Rupununi, Pomeroon — all carry Indigenous names, and an Amerindian feather headdress sits at the centre of Guyana's Coat of Arms.

Culture today: Each titled Amerindian village elects its own Toshao (chief), represented nationally by the National Toshaos Council. Indigenous Heritage Month is celebrated every September with heritage villages, craft fairs and traditional sports. Crafts like tibisiri basket-weaving, hammock-making and pottery, along with traditional dance and the home-grown Alleluia religion, remain very much alive today.

🌍

African-Guyanese

When they came: From the 1600s to 1834, enslaved Africans — mostly from the Akan, Igbo and Congo peoples of West and Central Africa — were forcibly brought by Dutch and later British planters to work the sugar plantations. Slavery was abolished in 1834, with full freedom in 1838.

Places they named: After emancipation, freed Africans pooled their savings to buy old plantations and turn them into free villages — Victoria (bought in 1839 by 128 formerly enslaved people, one of the first free villages in the Caribbean), Buxton, Beterverwagting, Golden Grove and Plaisance among them.

Food & culture: Cook-up rice, metemgee, pepperpot (Guyana's national dish, eaten especially at Christmas), foo-foo and souse. Culturally, Africans brought Mashramani-style masquerade bands, steel pan and Emancipation Day celebrations (1 August) marking the end of slavery.

🕉️

Indian-Guyanese

When they came: Indian indentured labourers began arriving on 5 May 1838, aboard the ships Whitby and Hesperus from Calcutta — an event still marked every year as Indian Arrival Day. Immigration continued until 1917, bringing over 238,000 Indians in total.

Places they named: Many former sugar-estate villages carry the imprint of Indian settlement, including Port Mourant (birthplace of President Cheddi Jagan), Albion, Whim and Skeldon in East Berbice-Corentyne, and Better Hope on the East Coast.

Food & culture: Curry and roti, dhal, seven curry, pholourie and dhal puri are now everyday Guyanese food. Diwali (the festival of lights) and Phagwah (Holi) are national public holidays, alongside Hindu and Muslim traditions, chutney and tassa drumming.

🏮

Chinese-Guyanese

When they came: The first Chinese indentured labourers arrived on 12–17 January 1853 aboard ships including the Glentanner and Lord Elgin, sailing from Amoy (Xiamen) in southern China. Around 14,000 Chinese arrived between 1853 and 1879.

Places they named: Hopetown, on the Demerara River, was founded in the 1860s as a self-governing Chinese settlement. In April 2026, the Guyana-China Friendship Park was opened on the West Bank Demerara, on the very land that received the first Chinese arrivals in 1853.

Food & culture: Chinese-Guyanese cooking — chow mein, fried rice and chop suey — is now everyday Guyanese food, found in Chinese restaurants across the country. Chinese New Year is widely celebrated, and many Guyanese of mixed African-Chinese heritage are known as "Chinee-dougla."

⛵

Portuguese-Guyanese

When they came: The first Portuguese indentured labourers — 40 peasants from the island of Madeira — arrived on 3 May 1835 aboard the ship Louisa Baillie, making them the first indentured workers brought in after the end of slavery, three years before the first Indian arrivals.

Places they named: By the 1840s many Portuguese had left the plantations to open small shops ("huckster" shops) across Georgetown and the villages, laying the foundation for much of Guyana's retail trade. Common Guyanese surnames like da Silva, Fernandes, Gomes and DeFreitas trace back to these Madeiran settlers.

Food & culture: Garlic pork, a marinated pork dish traditionally eaten at Christmas and New Year, is Guyana's best-known Portuguese food legacy, alongside a strong Roman Catholic tradition and wine-making heritage carried over from Madeira.
